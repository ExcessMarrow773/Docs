# Period 1 (1491–1607): Continuity and Change Analysis

This sorts the developments in the briefing by **type** (change vs. continuity), **magnitude** (how large the shift/persistence was), and **valence** (positive or negative — noted *by whom*, since almost every effect helped one group while harming another).

---

## Key Framing Note

Valence in this period is almost never universal. What was a **positive change for Europe** (new foods, new wealth) was frequently a **catastrophic change for Native Americans and Africans**. I've labeled valence by affected group rather than giving one blanket rating — this is also the kind of nuance that scores well on the AP written response rubrics.



---

## CHANGES — Sorted from Most to Least Transformative

| # | Change | Magnitude | Valence (by group) |
|---|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| 1 | **Permanent Atlantic contact established** (1492 onward) — two hemispheres previously isolated for millennia now permanently linked | **Transformative** | Mixed/contested — reshaped world history; net catastrophic for Native peoples, net expansionary for Europe |
| 2 | **Demographic collapse of Native Americans** via smallpox, measles, and other Old World diseases | **Transformative** | **Overwhelmingly negative** (Native Americans) — no immunity, massive population loss |
| 3 | **Rise of the transatlantic slave trade** — over 12 million Africans forcibly transported | **Transformative** | **Overwhelmingly negative** (Africans); economically positive for European/colonial elites |
| 4 | **Columbian Exchange of flora/fauna** — corn, potatoes, tomatoes to Europe; sugar cane, horses, pigs, iron tools to Americas | **Major** | Positive (Europe — population growth, dietary diversification); mixed (Americas — horses transformed Plains life, but exchange was framed/controlled by colonizers) |
| 5 | **Emergence of a rigid Spanish caste system** based on racial mixture | **Major** | **Negative** (Indigenous and African-descended peoples locked at bottom); reinforced Spanish elite power |
| 6 | **Shift of Native land control to European colonial control** | **Major** | **Negative** (Native Americans) |
| 7 | **Rise of European capitalism/trade economy** fueled by New World silver, population growth | **Major** | Positive (Europe, es. Spain's imperial wealth) |
| 8 | **Labor system shift**: encomienda → increasing reliance on the ascent/African slave system as Native labor declined | **Moderate–Major** | Negative for both Native and African populations (different forms of coerced labor, not a "solution" but a substitution) |
| 9 | **English-Native relations in places like Massachusetts**: shift from initial coexistence/trade to conflict and displacement | **Moderate** | Negative (Native Americans); the "shift" itself is a within-period change |
| 10 | **Introduction of syphilis to Europe** (New World disease moving eastward) | **Minor–Moderate** | Negative (Europe), though vastly smaller in scale than the Old World disease impact on the Americas |
| 11 | **Reputational transformation of Columbus** — failure (contemporary Spanish view) → national hero (19th century) → critically reassessed figure (post-1990s) | **Moderate** | N/A — a change in historical memory, not in the period itself, but useful for historiography questions |

## CONTINUITIES — Sorted from Strongest to Weakest

| # | Continuity | Strength | Valence (by group) |
|---|-----------|----------|---------------------|
| 1 | **Native political disunity** — decentralized, tribally loyal societies persisted throughout the period, meaning there was never a unified Native response to European expansion | **Strong** | **Negative** (Native Americans) — this continuity is precisely what limited effective resistance |
| 2 | **Use of religion to justify expansion and conquest** — true before contact (Catholic Reconquista) and remained true throughout (Protestant-Catholic missionary competition) | **Strong** | Negative (colonized peoples, as justification for exploitation); "positive" only from the self-interested European religious perspective |
| 3 | **Persistence of coerced/exploitative labor systems** — labor exploitation itself didn't disappear, only its source shifted (Native encomienda → African slavery) | **Strong** | **Negative** (both Native and African populations experienced continuous exploitation, just via different mechanisms) |
| 4 | **European competition for land, trade routes, and resources** — a continuous driver from the Age of Exploration's start through 1607 (Spain vs. Portugal vs. France vs. England) | **Strong** | Neutral/negative — fueled colonization pressure on Indigenous land throughout |
| 5 | **Native strategy of alliance-building** — tribes had long allied with or against each other; this continued, just with Europeans added as potential allies/enemies | **Moderate** | Neutral — a continuity in *strategy*, even as the players changed |
| 6 | **Environmental adaptation as the basis of Native regional cultures** (Southwest irrigation, Northwest fishing, Plains buffalo hunting) — largely unchanged through the early contact period | **Moderate** | Positive/neutral — reflects long-term cultural stability, though this stability would erode later in the colonial period |
| 7 | **European view of Native Americans as "exploitable" or "inferior"** — a continuous underlying assumption across Spanish, and later English, policy, even as specific practices differed | **Strong** | **Negative** (Native Americans) — persisted despite internal European debate (e.g., Las Casas vs. Sepúleda) |
