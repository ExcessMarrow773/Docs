# The Articles of Confederation
## **Organization of New Governments**
- Having declared independence, the 13 colonies now had to actually fight for it, and realized they needed some form of government to pull that off
- The challenge: getting 13 distinct colonies to unite, when the thing that really tied them together was just a shared distrust and fear of a tyrannical British government
- This led to an intentionally weak central government under the **Articles of Confederation**, written by the Second Continental Congress during the war
- While the war was going on, colony leaders were also busy turning their colonies into independently governed states, each with its own constitution
- Meanwhile, the same Congress that met in Philadelphia tried to define the powers of a brand new central government

## **State Governments**
- By 1777, ten of the former colonies had already written new constitutions
- Most were written *and* adopted by the states' own legislatures
- A few states (Maryland, Pennsylvania, North Carolina) actually put their proposed constitution to a vote of the people for ratification
- Each constitution was hotly debated between conservatives (wanted law and order) and liberals (wanted to protect individual rights and stop future tyranny)
- Even though they differed on specifics, the constitutions had some things in common:
	- **List of Rights**: every state constitution opened with a "bill" or "declaration" listing basic rights and freedoms, like the right to a jury trial and freedom of religion, that belonged to all citizens and that state officials couldn't infringe on
	- **Separation of Powers**: mostly, power was split into three branches, legislative (an elected two house legislature), executive (an elected governor), and judicial (a system of courts), meant to guard against tyranny, especially from a too powerful executive
	- **Voting**: the right to vote went to all White males who owned some property, based on the idea that property owners had more of a stake in government than poor, property less people
	- **Office holding**: people running for office usually needed an even higher property qualification than regular voters

## **The Articles of Confederation**
- In 1776, while Jefferson was busy writing the Declaration of Independence, **John Dickinson** drafted the first constitution for the country
- Congress tweaked Dickinson's plan to protect the powers of individual states, and the Articles of Confederation were adopted by Congress in 1777 and sent to the states for ratification
- **Ratification**
	- Got delayed by a fight over state claims to American Indian land west of the Alleghenies
	- Some states, like Rhode Island and Maryland, wanted everyone to give up those claims to the new central government
	- Once Virginia and New York finally agreed to give up their western land claims, the Articles were ratified in March 1781
- **Structure of Government**
	- Just one body, a congress, unicameral (one house)
	- Each state got one vote, and at least nine out of 13 votes were needed to pass important laws
	- No separate executive, no separate judiciary
	- Amending the Articles required a unanimous vote, which sounds like it would basically never happen
	- A **Committee of States** could make minor decisions when the full Congress wasn't in session
- **Powers**
	- Congress could wage war, make treaties, send diplomats, and borrow money
	- Could not regulate commerce or collect taxes, had to just rely on the states to donate money
	- No executive power to actually enforce laws either

## **The United States Under the Articles, 1781 to 1789**
- The states wanted the central government to be weak, and it definitely was, just a weak Congress with no executive or judicial branch at all

- **Accomplishments**
	- Independence: the government gets some credit for Washington's army winning and for negotiating decent terms in the peace treaty with Britain
	- **Land Ordinance of 1785**: set up a policy for surveying and selling western lands, and set aside one square mile of land in each 36 square mile township for public education
	- **Northwest Ordinance of 1787**: for the territory between the Great Lakes and the Ohio River, this set the rules for creating new states, granted limited self government to the territory, and prohibited slavery there
	- By setting land aside for schools and banning slavery, the government made the Northwest Territory attractive to both White and free Black settlers, though since land was auctioned off by the square mile (640 acres) at $1 an acre, the real benefit went first to whoever was rich enough to drop $640 at once, who would then resell smaller parcels to less wealthy Americans

- **Weaknesses of the Articles**
	- **Foreign Affairs**: European powers had little respect for a country that couldn't pay its debts or act united in a crisis, the government couldn't even enforce the Treaty of Paris, and was too weak to stop Britain from keeping military outposts on the frontier or restricting trade, or to force states to repay debts to foreigners and restore Loyalist property like the treaty required, and Britain and Spain both threatened to take advantage of this weakness
	- **Economic Problems**: Congress had no taxing power, could only ask states to donate money, and had no dependable way to repay war debt, states had their own unpaid debts too, which meant limited credit and less foreign trade, plus a bunch of states printed worthless paper money, all of which added up to an economic depression
	- **Internal Conflicts**: the 13 states basically treated each other as rivals, competing economically and slapping tariffs on goods crossing state lines, plus several states had boundary disputes, and the national government had no power to settle any of it
	- **Shays's Rebellion**: in summer 1786, **Captain Daniel Shays**, a Massachusetts farmer and Revolutionary War veteran, led other farmers in an uprising against high state taxes, imprisonment for debt, and lack of paper money, the rebels stopped tax collection and shut down debtors' courts, and in January 1787, when they tried to seize weapons from the Springfield armory, the Massachusetts state militia finally broke the rebellion

# The Constitutional Convention and Debates Over Ratification
## **The Annapolis Convention**
- To figure out what to do about the country's inability to solve its problems, **George Washington** hosted a conference at his home in **Mount Vernon**, Virginia, in 1785
- Representatives from Virginia, Maryland, Delaware, and Pennsylvania agreed the problems were serious enough to hold a bigger meeting at Annapolis, Maryland, and invited all the states
- Only five states actually sent delegates to the **Annapolis Convention** in 1786
- After talking about how to improve commercial relations between the states, **James Madison** and **Alexander Hamilton** convinced the others that another convention should happen in Philadelphia to revise the Articles of Confederation

## **Drafting the Constitution at Philadelphia**
- Once states elected delegates for the proposed Philadelphia convention, Congress gave its approval
- Called on all 13 states to send delegates "for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation"
- Only Rhode Island refused to send anyone, apparently they just didn't trust the other states

## **The Delegates**
- 55 delegates went to Philadelphia for the convention in summer 1787, all White, all male, mostly college educated
- Relatively young as a group (averaging their early forties), and with few exceptions, much wealthier than the average American of the time
- Well acquainted with law and politics, a bunch were practicing lawyers, many had helped write their own state constitutions
- First order of business was electing a presiding officer and deciding whether to talk to the public at all
- They voted to keep their meetings secret and say nothing until the work was done
- **George Washington** was unanimously elected chairperson
- **Benjamin Franklin**, the elder statesman at age 81, provided a calming, unifying influence
- The actual work of fashioning the Constitution's specific articles was directed by James Madison (who became known, despite his objections, as the "Father of the Constitution"), Alexander Hamilton, **Gouverneur Morris**, and **John Dickinson**
- These leaders represented different states but all shared the goal of strengthening the young nation
- Some major Revolutionary leaders were missing: **John Jay**, **Thomas Jefferson**, and **John Adams** were abroad on diplomatic business, and **Thomas Paine** was also in Europe
- **Samuel Adams** and **John Hancock** weren't chosen as delegates
- **Patrick Henry**, who opposed any growth in federal power, refused to take part at all

## **Key Issues at the Convention**
- The convention opened with delegates disagreeing on the whole point of the thing
- Some just wanted to revise the Articles, but strong nationalists like Madison and Hamilton wanted to write an entirely new document, arguing the confederate model (loosely united states under a weak central government) just didn't work
- They believed in **federalism**, a system with a strong but limited central government, and the nationalists quickly took control of the convention
- Americans in the 1780s generally distrusted government, even popularly elected officials, so Madison and other delegates believed in **separation of powers**, dividing power among different branches, with a system of **checks and balances** so each branch's power would be limited by the others

- **Representation**
	- Especially divisive: should bigger states like Virginia and Pennsylvania get proportionally more representatives than smaller states like New Jersey and Delaware
	- Madison's proposal, the **Virginia Plan**, favored the larger states
	- Countered by the **New Jersey Plan**, which favored the smaller states
	- Finally resolved by a compromise: **Roger Sherman** of Connecticut proposed the **Connecticut Plan**, also called the **Great Compromise**
	- Set up a bicameral (two house) Congress, with equal representation for each state in the Senate, but representation based on population in the House of Representatives

- **Slavery**
	- Two contentious issues came out of this
	- Should enslaved people count in a state's population? Southerners said yes, northerners said no since enslaved people didn't have the rights of citizens
	- Delegates agreed to the **Three Fifths Compromise**, counting each enslaved person as three fifths of a person for taxation and representation purposes
	- Should the slave trade even be allowed? Some wanted to ban it for humanitarian reasons, others worried about their labor supply
	- Delegates decided enslaved people could still be imported for at least 20 more years, until 1808, after which Congress could vote to abolish the practice if it wanted

- **Trade**
	- Northern states wanted the central government to regulate interstate and foreign commerce
	- The south worried export taxes would get slapped on agricultural products like tobacco and rice
	- The **Commercial Compromise** let Congress regulate interstate and foreign commerce, including tariffs on foreign imports, but banned any taxes on exports

- **The Presidency**
	- Debated the president's term of office, some thought the chief executive should serve for life
	- Delegates settled on a four year term with no limit on the number of terms
	- Also debated how to actually elect a president, and instead of a direct popular vote, decided to give each state electors equal to its total representatives plus senators, creating the **Electoral College**
	- This system existed because delegates were afraid too much democracy could lead to mob rule
	- Finally debated what powers to give the president, and decided to give them considerable power, including the power to veto acts of Congress

- **Ratification Procedure**
	- On September 17, 1787, after 17 weeks of debate, the Philadelphia convention approved a draft of the Constitution to send to the states
	- Anticipating pullback, the Framers specified in Article VII that only nine of the 13 states needed to approve it for ratification
	- Each state would hold its own popularly elected convention to debate and vote on it

## **Federalists and Anti Federalists**
- Ratification was fiercely debated for almost a year, September 1787 to June 1788
- Supporters of the Constitution and a strong federal government were **Federalists**
- Opponents who feared the new government would be too strong were **Anti Federalists**
- Federalists were most common along the Atlantic Coast and in big cities, while Anti Federalists tended to be small farmers and frontier settlers
- Federalists argued a stronger central government was needed to maintain order and preserve the Union, and pointed out the weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation, while portraying the Anti Federalists as just negative with no real solutions
- Anti Federalists argued a stronger central government would destroy the work of the Revolution, limit democracy, and restrict states' rights, and pointed out the proposed Constitution had no bill of rights, even claiming it gave the central government more power than the British ever had
- Federalists had the advantage of strong leaders and being well organized, plus widespread concern about the problems under the Articles
- Anti Federalists had the advantage of widespread distrust of government power from their experience as colonists, but were less united than the Federalists, and the Constitution itself was new, untried, and originally lacked a bill of rights

## **The Federalist Papers**
- A key part of the Federalist campaign was a series of persuasive essays written for a New York newspaper by James Madison, Alexander Hamilton, and John Jay
- 85 essays total, later published as a book called ***The Federalist Papers***, laying out reasons to believe in the practicality of each major part of the Constitution

## **The Path to Ratification**
- Federalists won early victories in Delaware, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania, the first three states to ratify
- Still weren't confident about enough other states, though
- **Debate on a Bill of Rights**
	- One of the Anti Federalists' biggest objections was that the Constitution didn't have a list of specific rights the federal government couldn't violate
	- They argued Americans fought the Revolutionary War to escape a tyrannical government in Britain, so what would stop a strong central government from doing the same thing
	- Federalists responded that since Congress would be elected by the people, they didn't need protecting from themselves, and that listing specific rights might let officials argue that unlisted rights could be violated
	- To win support anyway, the Federalists promised to add a bill of rights as the first order of business for the new Congress
- **Ratification Achieved**
	- With that promise, the Federalists addressed the Anti Federalists' biggest objection
	- New Hampshire voted yes in June 1788, giving Federalists the nine states needed for ratification
	- Still, the big states of Virginia and New York hadn't acted yet, and without them national unity would be in real trouble
- **Final States**
	- In 1788, Virginia was by far the most populous of the original 13 states
	- Anti Federalists there rallied behind **George Mason** and Patrick Henry, who saw the Constitution and a strong central government as a threat to hard won liberty
	- Virginia's Federalists, led by Washington, Madison, and **John Marshall**, won by a close vote, but only after promising a bill of rights
	- News of Virginia's vote, plus Alexander Hamilton's efforts, helped win over New York's ratifying convention
	- North Carolina (November 1789) and Rhode Island (May 1790) eventually reversed their earlier rejections, becoming the last two states to ratify the Constitution as the new "supreme law of the land"

# The Constitution
## **Federalism**
- The Framers hoped the newly ratified Constitution would follow Enlightenment ideals and provide a working system for the new republic
- Wanted to fix the weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation without giving the government too much power
- To guard against tyranny, they divided power vertically, between the federal and state levels, and horizontally, among three branches
- As a further check, and to fulfill a promise made during ratification, one of the first jobs of the new Congress was to propose a Bill of Rights
- The federal government handles issues affecting the whole country, like national defense and foreign affairs, and issues that cross state boundaries, like interstate commerce and a postal service
- States handle issues that only affect their own state, like schools and local elections
- As transportation, communication, and the economy have changed and increased interactions across state lines, the federal government has grown more powerful
- Constitutional amendments have also added specific powers to the federal government, for example the **19th Amendment**, ratified in 1920, gave Congress power to protect woman's right to vote
- By the 21st century, the government accounted for around 40 percent of the country's gross domestic product
- The federal government was usually responsible for over half of all public spending, paying for programs like Social Security, Medicare, and the military, and transferring money to state and local governments
- Most public employees, though, actually worked for state or local governments, especially in schools and universities

## **Separation of Powers**
- The Framers divided power among three main branches
	- **Legislative**: Congress makes laws, passes taxes, and allocates spending
	- **Executive**: led by the president, recommends and carries out laws and federal programs
	- **Judicial**: the Supreme Court and all the lower federal courts, interprets the laws and the Constitution
- The Constitution gave each branch ways to check or at least influence the other two
	- Congress can pass laws, but the president can veto them and the Supreme Court can rule them unconstitutional
	- The president can make treaties, but Congress has to ratify them
	- The president enforces laws, but the Supreme Court can stop those actions if they violate the Constitution
	- The Supreme Court interprets laws, but Congress can just write new ones
	- The Supreme Court can order a president to enforce a law, but the president has the power to appoint justices

## **The Bill of Rights**
- In 1789, the first Congress moved quickly to approve amendments defending individual liberty
- Drafted largely by James Madison, the ten ratified by the states in 1791 became known as the **Bill of Rights**
- Originally these only protected against abuses by the federal government
- Since the **14th Amendment** was ratified in 1868, most of these protections have also applied to abuses by state governments
- **First Amendment**: protects freedom of religion, speech, and the press, and the right to peaceably assemble and petition the government for a redress of grievances
- **Second Amendment**: protects the right to keep and bear arms, tied to the idea of a well regulated militia
- **Third Amendment**: says soldiers can't be quartered in a house without the owner's consent in peacetime, or outside of a manner prescribed by law in wartime
- **Fourth Amendment**: protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, and requires warrants to be based on probable cause
- **Fifth Amendment**: covers things like requiring a grand jury for serious crimes, protection against double jeopardy, the right not to testify against yourself, due process, and just compensation for private property taken for public use
- **Sixth Amendment**: guarantees the accused a speedy and public trial by an impartial jury, being informed of the accusation, confronting witnesses, and having a lawyer
- **Seventh Amendment**: guarantees a jury trial in civil suits over twenty dollars
- **Eighth Amendment**: bans excessive bail, excessive fines, and cruel and unusual punishment
- **Ninth Amendment**: says just because a right isn't listed in the Constitution doesn't mean people don't still have it
- **Tenth Amendment**: powers not given to the federal government, and not prohibited to the states, belong to the states or to the people
