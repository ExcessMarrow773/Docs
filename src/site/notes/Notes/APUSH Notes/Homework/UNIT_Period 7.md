# The Rise of Political Parties and the Era of Jefferson
## **The Election of 1800**
- Even though Washington warned against political parties, two groups showed up almost immediately: the **Federalists** (following Alexander Hamilton) and the **Democratic-Republicans** (following Thomas Jefferson)
- During Adams's presidency the Federalists rapidly lost popularity
	- People disliked the Alien and Sedition Acts
	- People complained about the new taxes to pay for a possible war with France
	- Adams avoided war, but he did convince Congress that building up the U.S. Navy was necessary
- **Establishment of Political Parties**
	- 1800 was the first election with a clear choice between political parties
	- Federalists wanted a stronger national government and leaned toward Great Britain
	- Democratic-Republicans emphasized states' powers and leaned toward the French
	- Both parties supported tariffs on imports to raise revenue, and tariffs would be the largest single source of federal revenue for the whole 19th century
	- The tariff debate split on regional lines: northern industrialists wanted higher tariffs to protect their companies, southerners relied on exporting cotton and other crops, so they wanted lower tariffs to encourage trade
- **Election Results**
	- Under the original Constitution each elector cast two votes for president, the winner became president and second place became vice president
	- In 1800 most electors voted for two Democratic-Republicans, one for **Thomas Jefferson** and one for **Aaron Burr**, so they tied
	- The House of Representatives had to break the tie, with each state getting one vote, and after days of debate they gave Jefferson the majority
	- Alexander Hamilton had urged his followers to back Jefferson, since he thought Jefferson was less dangerous and of higher character than Burr
	- Democratic-Republicans also took control of the House and Senate, so the Federalists were swept out of both the executive and legislative branches
- **A Peaceful Revolution**
	- Power passed from one party to another in 1801 without violence, which was rare for the time and a big sign the constitutional system would last
	- Federalists quietly accepted defeat and gave up control
	- This change is known as the **Revolution of 1800**

## **Jefferson's Presidency**
- In his first term Jefferson tried to win over Federalists by keeping Hamilton's national bank and debt repayment plan, and he continued the neutrality policies of Washington and Adams
- To keep his own party loyal, he stuck to limited central government by reducing the size of the military, cutting federal jobs, repealing excise taxes (including the whiskey tax), and lowering the national debt
- He appointed only Democratic-Republicans to his cabinet, hoping to avoid the internal divisions that distracted Washington
- His first four years were relatively free of discord compared to Adams's troubled administration

## **The Louisiana Purchase**
- The most important achievement of Jefferson's first term was buying the **Louisiana Territory**, a huge tract of western land where the Mississippi and Missouri rivers flow, little explored by Europeans
- The most valuable piece commercially was the port of **New Orleans** at the mouth of the Mississippi
- The territory was once French, then Spanish, but in 1800 **Napoleon Bonaparte** secretly forced Spain to give it back to France, hoping to restore the French empire in the Americas
- By 1803 Napoleon lost interest for two reasons: he wanted to focus on fighting Great Britain, and a rebellion led by **Toussaint Louverture** against French rule on Santo Domingo had caused heavy French losses
- **U.S. Interest in the Mississippi River**
	- The western frontier now stretched beyond Ohio and Kentucky into the Indiana Territory
	- Settlers there depended on rivers flowing into the Mississippi and down to New Orleans to transport goods
	- In 1802 Spanish officials still running New Orleans closed the port to Americans, revoking the right of deposit from the **Pinckney Treaty of 1795**
	- Frontier people demanded action, and Jefferson worried that as long as a foreign power controlled New Orleans, the U.S. risked getting tangled up in European affairs
- **Negotiations**
	- Jefferson sent ministers to France with orders to offer up to $10 million for New Orleans and a strip of land running east to Florida
	- If that failed, they were to start talks with Britain about an alliance
	- Napoleon's ministers, needing money for the war with Britain, offered the whole Louisiana Territory for $15 million
	- The American ministers were surprised, went beyond their instructions, and accepted
- **Constitutional Predicament**
	- Jefferson and most Americans loved the purchase, but Jefferson believed in a strict interpretation of the Constitution and rejected Hamilton's idea of implied powers
	- No clause in the Constitution said a president could buy foreign land
	- Jefferson set aside his idealism for the country's good and argued that adding land fell under the president's power to make treaties
	- Federalist senators criticized it, but the Democratic-Republican majority quickly ratified it
- **Consequences**
	- More than doubled the size of the U.S., removed a European presence from the nation's borders, and pushed the western frontier past the Mississippi
	- Strengthened Jefferson's hope for an agrarian society of independent farmers rather than Hamilton's urban, industrial vision
	- Boosted Jefferson's popularity and made the Federalists look like a weak, sectionalist (New England based) party that could only complain
- **Lewis and Clark Expedition**
	- Even before the purchase, Jefferson persuaded Congress to fund a scientific exploration of the West led by Captain **Meriwether Lewis** and Lieutenant **William Clark**
	- They set out from St. Louis in 1804, crossed the Rockies, reached the Oregon coast on the Pacific, and finished the return trip in 1806
	- Benefits: more geographic and scientific knowledge, stronger U.S. claims to Oregon Territory, better relations with American Indians, and better maps and routes for fur trappers and future settlers

## **Judicial Impeachments**
- Jefferson tried to undo Federalist measures and appointments, suspending the Alien and Sedition Acts and releasing people jailed under them
- Federalist judges appointed by Washington and Adams could only be removed by impeachment, which annoyed Jefferson, so he supported an impeachment campaign
- One federal district judge, found mentally unbalanced, was impeached by the House and removed by the Senate
- The House also impeached Supreme Court justice **Samuel Chase**, but the Senate acquitted him since there was no evidence of "high crimes"
- Otherwise the campaign mostly failed, though the threat of impeachment made judges more cautious and less partisan

## **Jefferson's Reelection**
- In 1804 Jefferson won in a landslide, getting all but 14 of 176 electoral votes
- His second term got harder: a plot by his former vice president Aaron Burr, a party split with a faction called the **"Quids"** accusing him of abandoning party principles, and trouble from the Napoleonic wars
- **Aaron Burr**
	- A Democratic-Republican caucus in 1804 decided not to nominate Burr for a second term as vice president
	- **Federalist Conspiracy**: Burr secretly teamed up with some radical New England Federalists, planning to win the New York governorship, unite New York with New England, and lead them to secede, but most Federalists followed Hamilton in opposing Burr, he lost, and the plot fell apart
	- **Duel with Hamilton**: Angered by an insulting remark attributed to Hamilton, Burr challenged him to a duel and fatally shot him, which cost the Federalists their last great leader and earned Burr lots of enemies
	- **Trial for Treason**: By 1806 Burr was plotting to take Mexico from Spain and maybe unite it with Louisiana under his rule, Jefferson ordered his arrest and trial, Chief Justice **John Marshall** (a longtime adversary of Jefferson) presided, and the jury acquitted Burr because of Marshall's narrow definition of treason and a lack of witnesses to any "overt act"

## **John Marshall's Supreme Court and Federal Power**
- John Marshall was one Federalist who kept major influence during the Democratic-Republican years, and his decisions consistently favored the central government and property rights over states' rights
- Ironically he was one of Jefferson's own cousins from Virginia
- Adams appointed him chief justice in the final months of his presidency, and he held the post for 34 years, influencing the Court as strongly as Washington influenced the presidency
- Even justices appointed by Democratic-Republican presidents often sided with him, since they were persuaded the Constitution created a federal government with strong and flexible powers
- **Influential Cases**
	- ***Marbury v. Madison* (1803)**: Just before leaving office Adams made "midnight appointments" of Federalist judges, but some commissions weren't delivered before Jefferson took over, so Jefferson told Secretary of State **James Madison** not to deliver them, and **William Marbury** sued for his commission. Marshall ruled Marbury had a right to it under the Judiciary Act of 1789, but that the act gave the Court more power than the Constitution allowed, so the law was unconstitutional and Marbury didn't get it. Marshall gave up a small Federalist gain for a huge long term win, establishing **judicial review**, the Court's power to decide whether acts of Congress or the president are allowed by the Constitution
	- ***Fletcher v. Peck* (1810)**: A land fraud case in Georgia, Marshall decided a state couldn't pass legislation invalidating a contract, the first time the Court declared a state law unconstitutional
	- ***Martin v. Hunter's Lease* (1816)**: The Court established it had jurisdiction over state courts in cases involving constitutional rights
	- ***Dartmouth College v. Woodward* (1819)**: New Hampshire tried to turn Dartmouth from a private college into a public one, and the Court struck down the law, arguing a state couldn't alter a private corporation's contract
	- ***McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819)**: Maryland tried to tax the Second Bank of the United States, and Marshall ruled a state can't tax a federal institution because "the power to tax is the power to destroy" and federal laws are supreme over state laws. He also settled the national bank debate with a loose interpretation, saying the Constitution gave the federal government the **implied power** to create one
	- ***Cohens v. Virginia* (1821)**: Two brothers were convicted in Virginia for illegally selling tickets for a congressionally authorized D.C. lottery, the Court upheld the conviction but established it could review state court decisions involving federal powers
	- ***Gibbons v. Ogden* (1821)**: New York granted a steamboat monopoly that conflicted with a congressional charter, and Marshall ruled the monopoly unconstitutional, establishing broad federal control of interstate commerce

## **Madison's Presidency**
- Jefferson followed Washington's precedent of retiring after two terms and backed his close friend Secretary of State **James Madison**
- **The Election of 1808**
	- Madison was widely seen as a brilliant thinker since leading the push to write and ratify the Constitution, and had worked hard with Jefferson building the Democratic-Republican Party
	- But he was a weak public speaker, stubborn, and lacked Jefferson's political skills
	- A congressional Democratic-Republican caucus nominated him, other party factions nominated two other candidates, but Madison still won a majority of electoral votes and beat both of them plus Federalist **Charles Pinckney**

# Politics and Regional Interests
## **The Era of Good Feelings**
- **James Monroe** was elected in 1816, less than two years after the last battle of the War of 1812, starting what a newspaper editorial called the "Era of Good Feelings," a term historians later adopted for his two terms
- It suggests nationalism, optimism, and goodwill, and in some ways that was true, since the Federalists faded away and the Democratic-Republicans adopted some of their policies and dominated politics
- But the unity was oversimplified, with heated debates over tariffs, the national bank, internal improvements, and public land sales, growing sectionalist tension over slavery, and Democratic-Republican factions that would soon split the party in two
- The actual "good feelings" may have only lasted from the election of 1816 to the **Panic of 1819**
- **James Monroe**
	- Fought in the Revolutionary War and suffered through the Valley Forge winter
	- Was prominent in Virginia politics, Jefferson's minister to Great Britain, and Madison's secretary of state
	- Continued the "Virginia dynasty," since four of the first five presidents were from Virginia (the other, John Adams, was from Massachusetts)
	- Beat Federalist **Rufus King** 183 electoral votes to 34 in 1816, and by 1820 got every electoral vote except one
	- With no organized opposition, he represented growing American nationalism, and under him the country acquired Florida, agreed on the Missouri Compromise, and adopted the Monroe Doctrine

## **Economic Nationalism**
- After the War of 1812, a political movement grew to support the economy, with internal improvements (roads and canals) and protecting young U.S. industries from European competition, though opinions often depended on what helped your own region
- **Tariff of 1816**
	- Before the war, tariffs were low and just for revenue, but during the war manufacturers built factories to replace British imports
	- In peacetime they feared British goods would be dumped on American markets, so Congress raised tariffs to protect manufacturers, the first **protective tariff** in U.S. history
	- New England, with little manufacturing then, was the only section to oppose it, while even the South and West supported it, believing it was needed for national prosperity
- **Henry Clay's American System**
	- **Henry Clay** of Kentucky proposed a plan with three parts: protective tariffs, a national bank, and internal improvements
	- Tariffs would promote manufacturing and raise money for a national transportation system, the bank would provide a national currency, and Clay said tariffs would mostly help the East, internal improvements would help the West and South, and the bank would help everyone
	- Two parts were in place by 1816, the last year of Madison's presidency: the protective tariff and the chartered **Second Bank of the United States** (the First Bank, Hamilton's idea, had its charter expire in 1811)
	- Madison and Monroe objected to internal improvements since the Constitution didn't explicitly allow spending federal money on roads and canals, and Monroe vetoed every such bill, so states had to do it themselves

## **The Panic of 1819**
- First major financial panic since the Constitution was ratified
- It happened after the Second Bank of the United States tightened credit to control inflation
- Many state banks closed, and unemployment, bankruptcies, and imprisonment for debt jumped
- The West was hit hardest, since many people were in debt from speculating on land after the war, and the Bank foreclosed on huge amounts of western farmland in 1819
- Nationalistic beliefs got shaken, and westerners started calling for land reform and strongly opposed both the national bank and debtors' prisons

## **Political Changes**
- The Federalist Party declined because it failed to adapt as the nation grew
- After opposing the War of 1812 and leading the secessionist **Hartford Convention**, it seemed out of step with the nationalism of the time, lost badly in 1816, and didn't even nominate a candidate in 1820
- **Changes in the Democratic-Republican Party**
	- As the only remaining national party, it faced internal strains
	- Members like **John Randolph** held onto limited government and strict interpretation of the Constitution
	- Most members adopted old Federalist ideas like a large army and navy and a national bank
	- Some flipped positions: **Daniel Webster** of Massachusetts strongly opposed the tariffs of 1816 and 1824 but supported even higher rates in 1828, and **John C. Calhoun** of South Carolina was an outspoken war hawk and nationalist in 1812 but championed states' rights after 1828
- Factions and sectional differences got more intense in Monroe's second term, and when Monroe honored the two term tradition, four other Democratic-Republicans ran in 1824, splitting the party (explained in Topic 4.8)

## **Western Settlement and the Missouri Compromise**
- In the ten years after the War of 1812 started, the population of settlers west of the Appalachians doubled, mostly between the Appalachians and the Mississippi, with some in the Louisiana Territory
- **Reasons for Westward Movement**
	- **Acquisition of Lands**: Victories over American Indians by **William Henry Harrison** in the Indiana Territory and **Andrew Jackson** in Florida and the South opened lots of territory for White settlers
	- **Economic Pressures**: The embargo and war hurt the Northeast so people left, and southern tobacco planters needed new land to replace exhausted soil, finding good cotton land in Alabama, Mississippi, and Arkansas
	- **Improved Transportation**: Roads, canals, steamboats, and railroads made reaching the frontier easier
	- **Immigrants**: More Europeans were attracted by speculators offering cheap land in the Great Lakes region and the Ohio, Cumberland, and Mississippi valleys
- **New Questions and Issues**
	- The new western states were small compared to the other sections, so western representatives bargained with other sections to boost their influence
	- Their main concerns were "cheap money" (easy credit) from state banks instead of the Bank of the United States, low federal land prices, and better transportation
	- On slavery they disagreed, since those settling the south wanted it for cotton labor and those settling the north had no use for it
	- In 1819 when the Missouri Territory applied for statehood, slavery became an angry debate

## **The Missouri Compromise**
- Since 1791 to 1792, when Vermont came in free and Kentucky came in slave, Congress tried to preserve a balance between North and South
- The House was hard to balance since the North's population grew faster, and by 1818 the North led 105 to 81, but the Senate was split evenly at 11 slave states and 11 free states, which let southern senators block legislation threatening their section
- Missouri's bid alarmed the North since slavery was well established there, so a slave state would tip the balance to the South
- Missouri was also the first part of the Louisiana Purchase to apply, so everyone worried about the status of future territories
- **Tallmadge Amendment**
	- Proposed by Representative **James Tallmadge** of New York, it would prohibit bringing more slaves into Missouri and require children of Missouri slaves to be freed at age 25
	- It would have gradually ended slavery in Missouri, and it was defeated in the Senate because enraged southerners saw it as the first step toward abolishing slavery everywhere
- **Clay's Proposals**
	- After months of heated debate, Henry Clay won majority support for three bills: admit **Missouri** as a slave state, admit **Maine** as a free state, and prohibit slavery in the rest of the Louisiana Territory north of latitude 36° 30΄
	- Both houses passed them and Monroe signed in March 1820, creating the **Missouri Compromise**
- **Aftermath**
	- Sectional feelings over slavery calmed after 1820, and the compromise preserved balance for more than 30 years and gave the nation time to mature
	- Still, the Era of Good Feelings was badly damaged, and Americans were torn between **nationalism** (loyalty to the Union) and **sectionalism** (loyalty to one's own region)

# America on the World Stage
## **Jefferson's Foreign Policy**
- Jefferson had real foreign affairs experience as a foreign minister in Europe and as secretary of state, which helped with the Louisiana Purchase but would be tested by lots of other problems
- **Difficulties Abroad**
	- Jefferson tried to avoid war as a matter of policy and principle, rejected permanent alliances, and tried to keep neutrality despite growing provocations from France and Britain during the **Napoleonic wars**
	- **Barbary Pirates**: The first big challenge came from piracy by the Barbary states on the North African coast. Washington and Adams had reluctantly paid tribute, the ruler of Tripoli demanded more from Jefferson, and Jefferson refused and sent a small Navy fleet to the Mediterranean. Sporadic fighting with Tripoli lasted four years (1801 to 1805), with no decisive victory, but the navy gained respect and some protection for U.S. ships
	- **Challenges to U.S. Neutrality**: France and Britain tried naval blockades of enemy ports and regularly seized neutral ships and cargoes, with Britain the worst offender since its navy dominated the Atlantic, and the most infuriating thing was **impressment**, capturing U.S. sailors the British claimed were British citizens and forcing them into the British navy
	- **Chesapeake-Leopard Affair**: In 1807, a few miles off Virginia, the British warship *Leopard* fired on the U.S. warship *Chesapeake*, killing three Americans and impressing four more. Anti-British feeling spiked and many wanted war, but Jefferson chose diplomacy and economic pressure
	- **Embargo Act (1807)**: Jefferson got Congress to ban American merchant ships from sailing to any foreign port, hoping Britain (with the U.S. as its largest trading partner) would stop violating neutral rights rather than lose trade. It backfired, hurting the U.S. more than Britain, since Britain easily got supplies from South America instead. The effect on the U.S. economy was devastating, especially for New England merchant marine and shipbuilders, and the depression was so bad that a movement to secede developed in New England. Jefferson called for repeal in 1809, though afterward U.S. ships could trade with all nations except Britain and France

## **President Madison's Foreign Policy**
- Madison faced the same European problems that plagued Jefferson's second term, and tried diplomacy plus economic pressure too, but unlike Jefferson he eventually took the U.S. to war
- **Commercial Warfare**
	- **Nonintercourse Act of 1809**: Americans could trade with all nations except Britain and France
	- **Macon's Bill No. 2 (1810)**: Introduced by **Nathaniel Macon**, it restored trade with Britain and France, but said that if either formally agreed to respect U.S. neutral rights, the U.S. would ban trade with that nation's foe
	- **Napoleon's Deception**: Napoleon announced he'd revoke the decrees violating U.S. rights, Madison took him at his word and embargoed trade with Britain in 1811, but Napoleon never meant it, and the French kept seizing American ships

## **The War of 1812**
- Neither Britain nor the U.S. wanted war, but it broke out anyway in 1812
- **Causes of the War**
	- **Free Seas and Trade**: The U.S. depended on free shipping across the Atlantic, but Britain and France didn't care about neutral rights while locked in a life and death struggle. Americans remembered Britain as a cruel enemy in the Revolution and France as a supporter, and Jeffersonians applauded France for overthrowing its monarchy. Both violated neutral rights, but the British were worse because of impressment
	- **Frontier Pressures**: Westerners wanted British Canada and Spanish Florida, with the British and their Indian and Spanish allies in the way. Shawnee brothers **Tecumseh** (a warrior) and **the Prophet** (a religious leader) tried to unite all the tribes east of the Mississippi to defend their lands. Settlers got suspicious and persuaded Indiana Territory governor **William Henry Harrison** to act, and at the **Battle of Tippecanoe** (1811) he destroyed the Shawnee headquarters, ending Tecumseh's confederacy effort. The British had only given a little aid, but frontier Americans blamed them for instigating it
	- **War Hawks**: The 1810 election brought young Democratic-Republicans to Congress, many from frontier states (Kentucky, Tennessee, Ohio), known as **war hawks** for wanting war with Britain. Led by Henry Clay and John C. Calhoun, they argued war was the only way to defend American honor, gain Canada, and destroy Indian resistance on the frontier
	- **Declaration of War**: British delays on neutral rights plus war hawk pressure convinced Madison to ask for war, and ironically in June 1812 the British had already agreed to suspend their blockade, but the news reached the White House after Congress declared war
- **A Divided Nation**
	- Congress barely supported it, with Pennsylvania and Vermont joining southern and western states for a slight majority, while most representatives from New York, New Jersey, and New England voted no
	- **Election of 1812**: Madison won reelection over **De Witt Clinton** of New York (backed by Federalists and antiwar Democratic-Republicans), with southern and western strength beating northern opposition
	- **Opposition to the War**: Opponents called it "Mr. Madison's War." The loudest critics were New England merchants (who profited after the Embargo Act's repeal, saw impressment as a minor inconvenience, and had commercial and Protestant ties to Britain), Federalist politicians (who saw it as a scheme to conquer Canada and Florida and boost Democratic-Republican votes), and the "Quids" (who said it violated limited federal power and peace)
- **Military Defeats and Naval Victories**
	- Facing Britain's naval power, Madison's strategists hoped for Napoleon's continued success in Europe and a U.S. land campaign against Canada
	- **Invasion of Canada**: A poorly equipped army launched a three part invasion in 1812 from Detroit, Niagara, and Lake Champlain, and they were easily repulsed. Burning government buildings in York (Toronto) in 1813 only encouraged British retaliation
	- **Naval Battles**: The U.S. navy won some notable victories thanks to superior shipbuilding and brave sailors, including many free African Americans. In late 1812 the USS **Constitution** ("**Old Ironsides**") sank a British ship off Nova Scotia, and American privateers captured lots of British merchant ships, but the British blockade of the U.S. coast crippled trade and fishing
	- **Battle of Lake Erie** (1813) was probably the most important naval battle, with Captain **Oliver Hazard Perry** declaring "We have met the enemy and they are ours." That led to Harrison's victory at the **Battle of the Thames** near Detroit, where Tecumseh was killed. In 1814 **Thomas Macdonough** beat a British fleet on Lake Champlain, forcing the British to abandon their plan to invade New York and New England
	- **Chesapeake Campaign**: By spring 1814 Napoleon's defeat let Britain send more forces. That summer a British army marched through Washington, D.C. and burned the White House, the Capitol, and other buildings. They tried to take Baltimore, but **Fort McHenry** held after a night of bombardment, which **Francis Scott Key** immortalized in "The Star-Spangled Banner"
	- **Southern Campaign**: General Andrew Jackson, in March 1814 at the **Battle of Horseshoe Bend** in present day Alabama, ended the power of the **Creek nation**, a British ally, opening land for White settlers. A British effort to control the Mississippi was stopped at New Orleans by Jackson leading frontier soldiers, free African Americans, and Creoles. The **Battle of New Orleans** (January 8, 1815) was impressive but meaningless, since it was fought two weeks after a treaty ending the war was signed at Ghent, but before the news arrived
- **The Treaty of Ghent**
	- By 1814 Britain was weary after fighting Napoleon for over a decade, and Madison's government knew it couldn't win decisively
	- American peace commissioners met British diplomats in Ghent, Belgium, and on Christmas Eve 1814 reached an agreement that halted fighting, returned conquered territory to prewar claimants, and recognized the prewar Canada border
	- It said nothing about the grievances that caused the war, with no British concessions on impressment, blockades, or maritime issues, so the war ended in stalemate with no gain for either side
- **The Hartford Convention**
	- Just before the war ended, radical New England Federalists, bitter about the war and the Democratic-Republican government, urged amending the Constitution and, as a last resort, voting on secession
	- A convention was held at Hartford, Connecticut in December 1814, where delegates rejected secession but adopted proposals to limit Democratic-Republican power in the South and West, including requiring a two thirds vote of both houses for any future war declaration
	- Soon after, news came of Jackson's victory and the Treaty of Ghent, ending criticism of the war and further weakening the Federalists as unpatriotic
- **The War's Legacy**
	- From Madison's view the war achieved none of its aims, but it had important consequences:
		1. Surviving two wars with Britain earned the U.S. respect from other nations
		2. The U.S. accepted Canada as part of the British Empire
		3. The Federalist Party ended as a national force and declined even in New England
		4. New England's talk of nullification and secession set a precedent the South would later use
		5. Abandoned by the British, American Indians had to give up land to White settlement
		6. The British blockade limited European goods, so U.S. factories were built and Americans moved toward industrial self sufficiency
		7. War heroes like Jackson and Harrison would soon lead a new generation of politicians
		8. Nationalism grew stronger, along with a belief that the future lay in the West and away from Europe

## **Monroe and Foreign Affairs**
- After the war the U.S. took a more aggressive, nationalistic approach, and when Barbary pirate problems returned in Madison's term, a fleet under **Stephen Decatur** was sent in 1815 to force North African rulers to allow free American shipping in the Mediterranean
- Monroe and Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams** kept a nationalistic policy that advanced American interests while keeping peace
- **Canada**
	- The Treaty of Ghent left most diplomatic differences unresolved, including many about Canada
	- **Rush-Bagot Agreement (1817)**: A major disarmament pact strictly limiting naval armament on the Great Lakes, later extended to border fortifications, and the border would become the longest unfortified border in the world
	- **Treaty of 1818**: Shared fishing rights off Newfoundland, joint occupation of Oregon Territory for ten years, and the northern limit of the Louisiana Territory set at the 49th parallel, establishing the western U.S. and Canada boundary
- **Florida**
	- During the War of 1812 U.S. troops occupied western Florida, which Spain (Britain's ally) had held
	- After the war Spain struggled to govern the rest of Florida because its troops left to fight revolts in South America
	- The chaos let Seminoles, runaway slaves, and White outlaws raid U.S. territory and retreat across the border, giving Monroe and Andrew Jackson an excuse for military action in a territory American expansionists had long wanted
	- **Jackson's Military Campaign**: In late 1817 Monroe told Jackson to stop the raiders and, if needed, pursue them into Spanish west Florida. Jackson went at it with a vengeance and probably beyond his orders, leading militia into Florida in 1818, destroying Seminole villages, hanging two Seminole chiefs, capturing Pensacola, driving out the Spanish governor, and hanging two British traders accused of helping the Seminoles
	- Many in Congress feared war with Spain and Britain, but John Quincy Adams persuaded Monroe to back Jackson and the British decided not to step in
	- **Florida Purchase Treaty (1819)**: Spain, worried the U.S. would seize Florida and busy with Latin America, handed over all its Florida possessions and its Oregon claims, and in exchange the U.S. assumed $5 million in claims against Spain and gave up claims to Texas. Also called the **Adams-Onís Treaty**
- **The Monroe Doctrine**
	- After Napoleon fell in 1815, restored monarchies in France, Austria, and Prussia, with Russia, worked to suppress liberal movements in Italy and Spain and considered helping Spain regain South America, where several republics had declared independence
	- Russia's presence in Alaska also worried Britain and the U.S., since Russian seal hunters used Alaskan trading posts as a base and spread south to a post at San Francisco Bay, and British and U.S. leaders saw a shared interest in protecting the Americas
	- **British Initiative**: British naval power deterred Spain, but protecting trade with Latin American republics required diplomacy, so British Foreign Secretary **George Canning** proposed to U.S. minister **Richard Rush** a joint Anglo-American warning against European intervention in South America
	- **American Response**: Monroe and most advisers liked it, but John Quincy Adams disagreed, believing joint action would limit U.S. expansion opportunities, and reasoned that if the U.S. acted alone Britain would back it up anyway, and no European power would risk war in South America with the British navy around. Monroe followed Adams
	- **The Doctrine**: On December 2, 1823 Monroe put into his annual message to Congress that the American continents were no longer open to future colonization by any European power, and that the U.S. opposed European interference in the affairs of any republic in the Western Hemisphere
	- **Impact**: Americans applauded it but soon forgot it since they cared more about domestic issues. Canning was annoyed since it applied to Britain too, and European monarchs reacted angrily but knew the British navy, not Monroe's words, thwarted them. It mattered more in later decades, when it was hailed as the cornerstone of U.S. policy toward Latin America, and in the 1840s President **James Polk** was the first of many presidents to cite it
