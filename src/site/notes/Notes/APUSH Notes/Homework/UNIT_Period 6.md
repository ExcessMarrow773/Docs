# Shaping a New Republic
## **Washington's Presidency**
- The first Congress under the Constitution was elected in 1788 and held its first session in March 1789 in New York City, the nation's temporary capital at the time
- Everyone just assumed **George Washington** would be the electoral college's unanimous pick for president, and yep, he was

## **Organizing the Federal Government**
- Washington took the oath of office as the first U.S. president on April 30, 1789
- From then on, what the Constitution's checks and balances actually meant in practice would get worked out day by day through the decisions of Congress, the president, and the Supreme Court
- **Executive Departments**
	- Washington's first job as chief executive was organizing new departments of the executive branch
	- The Constitution lets the president appoint department heads, but the Senate has to confirm them
	- Washington's four appointments: **Thomas Jefferson** as secretary of state, **Alexander Hamilton** as secretary of the treasury, **Henry Knox** as secretary of war, and **Edmund Randolph** as attorney general
	- These four formed a cabinet of advisers Washington met with regularly, and presidents still do this today
- **Federal Court System**
	- The only federal court actually named in the Constitution is the Supreme Court
	- Congress got the power to create other federal courts and decide how many justices sit on the Supreme Court
	- One of Congress's first laws was the **Judiciary Act of 1789**, setting up a Supreme Court with one chief justice and five associate justices
	- This court could rule on the constitutionality of state court decisions, and the act also set up 13 district courts and three circuit courts of appeals

## **Hamilton's Financial Program**
- One of the biggest problems left over from the Articles of Confederation was the government's financial mess
- Hamilton, as treasury secretary, presented Congress with a plan for stabilizing U.S. finances, with three main parts:
	1. Pay off the national debt at face value and have the federal government assume the states' war debts
	2. Protect the young nation's "infant" industries and raise revenue by putting high tariffs on imported goods
	3. Create a national bank for depositing government funds and printing banknotes to back a stable U.S. currency
- Support mostly came from northern merchants, who'd benefit directly from high tariffs and a stable currency
- Opponents included the Antifederalist, worried states would lose power as the central government gained it, and Thomas Jefferson led a faction of southern Antifederalist who saw Hamilton's plan as only helping the rich at the expense of indebted farmers
- After a lot of political wrangling, Congress adopted Hamilton's plan in a slightly modified form (the tariffs ended up lower than Hamilton wanted)
- **Debt**
	- Jefferson and his supporters agreed to let the federal government pay off the national debt at face value and assume the states' war debts
	- In exchange, Hamilton agreed to Jefferson's idea of putting the nation's capital in the south along the Potomac River, which after Washington's death would be named Washington, D.C.
- **National Bank**
	- Jefferson argued the Constitution didn't actually give Congress the power to create a bank
	- Hamilton took a broader view, arguing the "necessary and proper" clause let Congress do whatever was needed to carry out its listed powers
	- Washington sided with Hamilton, and the bank got voted into law
	- Although chartered by the federal government, the **Bank of the United States** was privately owned, and as a major shareholder, the federal government could print paper currency and use federal deposits to stimulate business

## **Foreign Affairs Under Washington**
- Washington's first term (1789 to 1793) lined up with the outbreak of the French Revolution, which set off a series of wars between the new French Republic and the monarchies of Europe
- Basically his whole presidency, and Adams's after him, was wrapped up in the question of whether to support France, France's enemies, or neither
- **French Revolution**
	- Americans generally rooted for the French people trying to set up a republic, but a lot were also horrified by the mob violence and mass executions
	- The alliance between the U.S. and France technically was still in effect, but it had been with the French monarchy, not this new revolutionary republic
	- Jefferson and his supporters sympathized with the revolutionaries, and argued that since Britain was seizing American ships headed to French ports, the U.S. should join France against Britain
- **Proclamation of Neutrality (1793)**
	- Washington thought the young nation wasn't strong enough to get into a European war
	- Despite popular pressure, he issued a proclamation of U.S. neutrality in 1793
	- Jefferson resigned from the cabinet over disagreeing with this policy
- **"Citizen" Genêt**
	- "Citizen" **Edmond Genêt**, the French minister to the U.S., objected to Washington's neutrality and broke diplomatic norms by appealing directly to the American people to support France
	- His conduct was so outrageous that even Jefferson agreed with Washington's request to have him removed
	- Genêt got recalled by his government but chose to stay in the U.S. anyway, where he married and became a citizen

## **The Jay Treaty (1794) with Great Britain**
- Washington sent Chief Justice **John Jay** on a special mission to Britain to deal with two issues: Britain's ongoing occupation of posts on the U.S. western frontier, and Britain's practice of searching and seizing American ships and impressing sailors into the British navy
- After a year of negotiations, Jay came back with a treaty where Britain agreed to evacuate its posts, but the treaty said nothing about impressment
- Narrowly ratified by the Senate, the unpopular **Jay Treaty** angered American supporters of France, but it did keep Washington's neutrality policy intact and kept the U.S. at peace

## **The Pinckney Treaty (1795) with Spain**
- The Jay Treaty had a totally unexpected effect on Spain's policy toward its American territories
- Spain saw the treaty as a sign the U.S. might be getting closer to Britain, so it decided to consolidate its own holdings in North America
- Spain was worried about its colonies in the southeast, and **Thomas Pinckney**, the U.S. minister to Spain, negotiated a treaty with these terms:
	- Spain opened the lower Mississippi River and New Orleans to American trade
	- Americans got the right of deposit, letting them transfer cargo in New Orleans without paying Spanish duties
	- Spain accepted that Florida's northern boundary should be at the 31st parallel, not further north like they'd previously insisted

## **Domestic Concerns Under Washington**
- Besides foreign challenges and stabilizing credit, Washington also faced a bunch of domestic problems
- **American Indians**
	- Through the late 1700s, settlers kept crossing the Alleghenies and pushing the frontier into the Ohio Valley and beyond
	- To resist this, a number of tribes formed the **Northwest (or Western) Confederacy**, with the Shawnee, Delaware, Iroquois, and others allying under Miami war chief **Little Turtle**
	- They initially won a bunch of bloody victories over settler militias
	- The British were sometimes supplying arms and encouraging attacks on settlers, which made Americans furious
	- In 1794, the U.S. army under General **Anthony Wayne** defeated the Confederacy tribes at the **Battle of Fallen Timbers** in northwestern Ohio
	- The next year, the defeated chiefs agreed to the **Treaty of Greenville**, giving up claims to the Ohio Territory and opening it to settlement
- **The Whiskey Rebellion (1794)**
	- To make up the revenue lost from tariffs being lower than he wanted, Hamilton got Congress to pass excise taxes, especially on whiskey
	- In western Pennsylvania, a group of farmers refused to pay the federal whiskey tax, which seemed like a real threat to the government's viability under the Constitution
	- The farmers couldn't really afford the tax on the whiskey they distilled from surplus corn, so instead of paying it, they attacked revenue collectors to defend their "liberties"
	- Washington responded by federalizing 15,000 state militia and putting them under Hamilton's command
	- The show of force worked, the rebellion collapsed with almost no bloodshed, and the federal government's authority got a lot stronger
	- Some Americans praised Washington's response, contrasting it with the previous government's helplessness during Shays's Rebellion
	- Westerners, though, widely resented the military action as an unwarranted use of force against ordinary people, and Jefferson, the government's chief critic, gained popularity as a champion of western farmers
- **Western Lands**
	- In the 1790s, the Jay Treaty and the win at Fallen Timbers gave the federal government control of huge tracts of land
	- Congress encouraged fast settlement by passing the **Public Land Act** in 1796, setting up orderly procedures for dividing and selling federal land at moderate prices
	- The process of adding new states to the Union went smoothly, starting with Vermont in New England in 1791, then Kentucky in 1792 and Tennessee in 1796 reflecting the westward push

## **The First Political Parties**
- Washington's unanimous election in 1789 reinforced the popular belief that political parties weren't needed
- The Constitution didn't mention political parties at all, and the Framers assumed none would form, but they were quickly proven wrong
- The Federalist versus Antifederalist debates in 1787 and 1788 were the first sign a two-party system would become a core part of American politics

## **Origins**
- In colonial times, legislators formed temporary factions that voted together on a specific policy, then dissolved once it was settled
- The Federalist versus Antifederalist fight over ratification closely resembled these older factional disputes
- What was unusual was that this conflict was organized, at least by the Federalists, across state lines, which foreshadowed the national parties that came soon after
- In the 1790s, sometimes called the Federalist era since Federalist policies dominated, parties began forming around two leading figures: Hamilton and Jefferson
- The **Federalist Party** backed Hamilton and his financial program
- The **Democratic-Republican Party** backed Jefferson and tried to elect candidates across different states who opposed Hamilton's program
- The French Revolution further solidified the parties, as Americans split sharply over whether to support France, with a lot of people following Jefferson's lead in openly challenging Washington's neutrality policy

## **Differences Between the Parties**
- The Federalists were strongest in the northeastern states and wanted more federal power
- The Democratic-Republicans were strongest in the south and on the western frontier and argued for states' rights
- By 1796, both parties were taking shape and getting better organized, and that same year Washington announced he'd retire to private life after his second term
- **Federalists**: led by John Adams and Alexander Hamilton, interpreted the Constitution loosely, wanted a strong central government, were pro-British, wanted a large peacetime army and navy, aided business, created a national bank, supported high tariffs, and drew support mostly from northern business owners and large landowners
- **Democratic-Republicans**: led by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, interpreted the Constitution strictly, wanted a weak central government, were pro-French, wanted a small peacetime army and navy, favored agriculture, opposed a national bank and high tariffs, and drew support mostly from skilled workers, small farmers, and plantation owners

## **Washington's Farewell Address**
- With Alexander Hamilton's help, the retiring president wrote his **Farewell Address**, published in newspapers in late 1796
- Had huge influence because of Washington's prestige
- He warned against a few things:
	- Don't get involved in European affairs
	- Don't make "permanent alliances" in foreign affairs
	- Don't form political parties
	- Don't fall into sectionalism
- For the next century, future presidents mostly listened to his first two warnings against foreign entanglements
- On political parties, though, Washington was already behind the times, since parties were already becoming a core part of the American political system, and sectional differences were only growing
- One long term effect of Washington leaving office after two terms was that later presidents followed his example, presidents elected to two terms (Jefferson, Madison, Monroe, Jackson) voluntarily retired even though the Constitution didn't limit a president's time in office
- This two term tradition held unbroken until 1940, when **Franklin Roosevelt** won a third term
- Then the **22nd Amendment**, ratified in 1951, made the two term limit official in the Constitution

## **John Adams's Presidency**
- Even while Washington was writing his Farewell Address, political parties were working to win majorities in Congress and line up enough electors to pick the next president
- Vice President **John Adams** was the Federalists' candidate, former secretary of state **Thomas Jefferson** was the Democratic-Republicans' pick
- Adams won by three electoral votes
- Jefferson became vice president, since the original Constitution gave that job to whoever came in second in electoral votes (this changed with the **12th Amendment** in 1804, after which president and vice president ran as a team)
- **The XYZ Affair**
	- Troubles related to the French Revolution gave Adams his first major challenge as president
	- Americans were angry that French warships and privateers were seizing U.S. merchant ships
	- Adams sent a delegation to Paris to try to work things out peacefully
	- Certain French ministers, known only as X, Y, and Z since their names were never revealed, asked for bribes before they'd even start negotiating
	- The American delegates refused, and news of the demands infuriated Americans, who started clamoring for war with France
	- "Millions for defense, but not one cent for tribute" became the slogan of the moment
	- One faction of the Federalist Party, led by Hamilton, hoped a war could win the U.S. French and Spanish land in North America
	- Adams resisted the popular push for war, knowing the U.S. Army and Navy weren't strong enough yet to fight a major power, and instead sent new ministers to Paris
- **The Alien and Sedition Acts**
	- Anger at France helped Federalists win a majority in both houses of Congress in the 1798 elections
	- Federalists used this win to pass laws restricting their political opponents, the Democratic-Republicans
	- Since most immigrants voted Democratic-Republican, Federalists passed the **Naturalization Act**, raising the years required for citizenship from 5 to 14
	- They also passed the **Alien Acts**, letting the president deport aliens considered dangerous and detain enemy aliens during wartime
	- Most seriously, they passed the **Sedition Act**, which made it illegal for newspaper editors to criticize the president or Congress, with fines or imprisonment for violators
- **The Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions**
	- Democratic-Republicans argued the Alien and Sedition Acts violated the 1st Amendment
	- In 1799, the Supreme Court hadn't yet established judicial review (the idea it could overturn laws that conflicted with the Constitution)
	- So Democratic-Republican leaders challenged the Federalist Congress by passing nullifying resolutions in their own state legislatures
	- The Kentucky legislature adopted a resolution written by Thomas Jefferson, and the Virginia legislature adopted one introduced by James Madison
	- Both declared that states had entered into a "compact" when forming the national government, so if the federal government broke that compact, a state could nullify the federal law
	- Only Kentucky and Virginia actually adopted these resolutions in 1799, but the argument would get reused a lot in the nullification controversy of the 1830s
	- The crisis faded once Federalists lost control of Congress after the 1800 election, and the Democratic-Republican majority let the acts expire or repealed them
	- In 1803, the Supreme Court under Chief Justice **John Marshall** asserted its own power to decide whether federal laws were constitutional

# Developing an American Identity
## **Social Change**
- A truly unique American identity took at least a generation to become established
- Admiration of the "founding fathers," the leaders who declared independence, fought a war, and created a new government, became the core of an American identity that continues today
- George Washington's Farewell Address and two term tradition are a clear example of this ongoing influence
- The fact that people still debate the founding fathers' words and ideas shows how important they are to understanding the country
- Americans retained a lot of their ancestors' culture and traditions while also forging a new identity, built on the foundation of the 13 colonies, shaped by the Revolution, and expanded by regional differences and ongoing immigration
- The War for Independence profoundly changed American society, not just its politics
- Some changes happened right before the war ended, others evolved gradually as revolutionary ideas filtered down to regular people
- Together these changes made Americans more aware of how the U.S. was different from Great Britain and the rest of Europe
- **Abolition of Aristocratic Titles**
	- State constitutions and laws abolished old institutions from medieval Europe
	- No legislature could grant titles of nobility, and no court could recognize primogeniture (the first born son's right to inherit the family's property)
	- Whatever aristocracy existed in colonial America was weakened further by the confiscation of large Loyalist estates, many of which got subdivided and sold to raise money for the war
- **Separation of Church and State**
	- Most states adopted separation of church and state, refusing to give financial support to any religious group
	- The Anglican Church (which became the Episcopal Church in the U.S.) had been closely tied to the king's government, but it lost state support (was disestablished) in the south
	- Only in three New England states, New Hampshire, Connecticut, and Massachusetts, did the Congregational Church keep getting state support through a religious tax, and this finally stopped in New England in the early 1830s
- **Regional Variations**
	- Not all states changed at the same time, and the regional differences from the colonial period kept shaping how states evolved
	- The term "southerner" had come into common use by the 1780s
	- The biggest difference was slavery, which kept declining in northern states while getting stronger than ever in southern states
	- Visitors noticed behavioral differences too, in 1785 Jefferson wrote to a friend in France that a careful visitor could tell the line of latitude just by paying attention to how people acted, with northerners more serious and hardworking, and southerners more generous and forthright in speech

## **Political Change**
- The rise of political parties both added to and reflected the American identity
- The distinctions between the two original parties, Federalist and Democratic-Republican, came from the Federalist versus Antifederalist debate over ratifying the Constitution
- These distinctions matured based mostly on regional differences and different views of the federal government's proper role
- The evolution of political parties, and the distinctiveness of American identity, continues today

## **Cultural Change**
- While a lot of the nation's culture still reflected its British origins, a distinctive national identity gradually evolved
- This was helped along by the expansion of newspapers in the late 1700s as a way to communicate and discuss politics
- Writer **Charles Brockden Brown** explored the meaning of an American identity through novels
- In Philadelphia, **Charles Wilson Peale** opened what's recognized as the first art gallery
- In the 1790s, **Pierre-Charles L'Enfant** designed Washington, D.C., **Gilbert Stuart** painted the nation's leaders, and the American Academy of Fine Arts held its first exhibition
- Later developments, like the first dictionary for American English and a book on American geography, kept building this distinctive culture

# Movement in the Early Republic
## **Migration and Settlement**
- Founding the Republic increased the movement of people, mostly westward
- The uncertainty of rebellion and war had ended, and peace and the removal of British control brought a reordering of government and life
- Still, hostile forces remained on the young nation's borders, the British to the north and west, the Spanish to the south and west, and Native Americans, both within and on the borders, who resented settlers expanding onto their lands
- People moving west, whether born in America or arriving as free immigrants, indentured servants, or enslaved Africans, faced a range of forces, both friendly and hostile
- This movement was recognized and accepted from the very start of the nation
- The **Northwest Ordinance**, enacted under the Articles of Confederation, provided a mechanism for migration and settlement, planning for the sale of government land, orderly admission of western territory as new states, public education, and outlawing slavery in the territory
- While the government had foreseen migration, it still couldn't eliminate the disputes and conflicts that came with it

## **American Indians**
- By the end of the 18th century, Native Americans found themselves losing conflicts with settlers, and as a result were increasingly living on reservations or forced to migrate west
- **Laws**
	- In 1790, the **Indian Intercourse Act** was one of the first laws passed by the new nation
	- Placed the federal government, not the states, in control of all legal actions with Native Americans, including purchasing their land and regulating trade and travel over their lands
	- These laws were largely ignored by traders and settlers moving westward
- **Resistance**
	- Settlers ignored laws and treaties meant to keep peace with American Indians, and the government usually backed the settlers once disputes turned violent
	- In the Northwest Territory in the 1790s, a confederation of Shawnee and other American Indians twice defeated government troops
	- In response, a larger government force defeated the confederation at the **Battle of Fallen Timbers** in what's now northwestern Ohio
	- The American Indian position was further weakened as the British, who had been supporting them, gradually closed the trading forts they'd maintained since right after the Revolution
- **West of the Mississippi**
	- Migration was a survivable option for many tribes, who faced overwhelming force, fatal foreign diseases, and destruction of their hunting grounds
	- Some tribes stayed where their ancestors had settled, unwilling to leave despite being surrounded by hostile settlers
	- The Iroquois stayed on reservations or moved north into Canada
	- Many, including the Shawnee in the north and the Cherokee further south, moved across the Mississippi River, and these journeys were dangerous since the tribes native to those western lands resisted the incursions
- **The Southern Frontier**
	- Further south, near New Orleans and in Florida, the Spanish were mainly focused on stopping U.S. settlers from moving in, so they allowed Native Americans more freedom

## **Population Change**
- The population grew for several reasons
	- Europeans kept immigrating to the U.S., though in small numbers, with the flow rising and falling based on political and economic upheavals in Europe
	- Enslaved Africans kept being brought into the country, since slaveowners knew the Constitution allowed this trade to end after 1808
	- The biggest population gain was natural, births exceeding deaths, tied to a plentiful food supply and families wanting kids who could help on the farm
- The westward movement was helped along by scouts and early settlers who blazed trails through the wilderness, like the legendary **Daniel Boone**, who led the way across the Appalachian Mountains and helped establish the early White settlements in the old northwest

## **Slavery**
- By the late 18th century, some people openly opposed slavery, many of them Quakers, Mennonites, or others motivated by their Christian faith
- Some were influenced by Enlightenment ideals about equality and liberty and didn't see a place for slavery in a democratic republic
- Many people, including slaveowners like **James Madison**, disliked slavery and hoped it would fade away like it was starting to in Europe and parts of Latin America, believing increasing immigration would provide low cost free labor to replace enslaved workers
- **Cotton**
	- Slavery grew instead of declining starting in 1793, when **Eli Whitney** invented the **cotton gin**, a device for separating cotton fiber from the seeds
	- This turned a slow, costly process into a quick, cheap one, transforming southern agriculture
	- Growing cotton suddenly became hugely profitable, and demand for enslaved African Americans shot up
	- Mechanization of the textile industry also increased cotton's value
	- The British mechanized first and passed laws to keep their factory designs from leaving the country
	- A young apprentice named **Samuel Slater** broke that law anyway, memorized a factory design, moved to the U.S., and built his own factory, launching a new, more efficient American textile industry
	- Together, the cotton gin and textile mechanization made cotton cloth cheaper and more plentiful than ever, turning cotton goods production into a major global industry
- **Conflict Over Expansion of Slavery**
	- Within the U.S., plantation owners eager to grow more cotton with enslaved labor looked west for more land
	- After 1800, they quickly settled Alabama and Mississippi, which both had great climate and geography for cotton
	- Their desire for land further west and north soon ran into resistance, as more northerners opposed slavery, or hoped to settle that land themselves without competing against enslaved labor, reflecting a growing regional conflict over slavery
- **The Movements of Enslaved African Americans**
	- Some enslaved people managed to escape bondage, finding freedom by reaching a free northern state, though the Constitution had a clause requiring states to return fugitives to their owners
	- Some escaped to Canada, more settled on land controlled by Native Americans or in Florida, which belonged to Spain until 1821
	- Most enslaved people who moved, though, did so because their owners were chasing greater profits
	- By the 1790s, Chesapeake planters had more enslaved people than they needed, as a decline in the uncertain tobacco market combined with a growing enslaved population (through natural increase and continued importation) created a surplus
	- Efforts to train enslaved people in skilled trades or lease them out as servants in growing cities didn't satisfy owners financially, and moving enslaved people from fields to towns, where they could more easily board boats and carriages, raised the risk of escape to the north
	- The growing demand for cotton field labor gave Chesapeake planters a new opportunity, selling enslaved African Americans to cotton planters in newly settled lands further south and west, like Alabama and Mississippi
	- This interregnal slave trade became huge, moving between 500,000 and 1 million people before the Civil War began in 1861
	- This trade was especially cruel because it often broke families apart, and many enslaved people who were sold never saw their parents, children, or other relatives again
